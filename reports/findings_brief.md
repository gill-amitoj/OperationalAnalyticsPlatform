# Edmonton Traffic Safety: What the Collision Data Shows

**Briefing note: independent analysis of City of Edmonton open data (Annual Collision Report, 2010–2023)**

## Summary

Edmonton recorded **20,039 collisions in 2023**. **325** of them were fatal or caused serious injury, and **24 people were killed**.
Serious harm per resident fell by two-thirds between 2010 and 2020, but it has risen since. Most collisions happen in
the afternoon rush, but the most severe ones happen late at night, and a small set of intersections tops the City's
high-collision list every year.

## What the data shows

1. **Late-night collisions are almost four times as likely to kill or seriously injure someone.**
   Between midnight and 5 a.m., **40.5 of every 1,000 collisions** were fatal or serious, compared with **10.9 per 1,000**
   between 7 a.m. and 6 p.m. These hours have only **3.3% of collisions** but **20 of the 72 fatal collisions (28%)**
   from 2019 to 2023. The gap appears in every year (36–47 vs 9.5–12.4 per 1,000).

2. **The afternoon rush is when collisions are most frequent.**
   **28% of all collisions (26,680)** happen in the three hours from 3 to 6 p.m., consistently each year (27.4–28.7%).
   The busiest single hour of the week is **Friday 4–5 p.m.** (1,763 collisions over five years).

3. **The same intersections keep appearing on the high-collision list.**
   **26 locations, 23 of them intersections,** were on the City's published top-collision list in all five years
   2019–2023, together accounting for **5,503 collisions**. **107 Avenue NW & 142 Street NW** ranked #1 or #2 in four
   of five years and recorded **169 collisions in 2023**, the highest single-site count in any year of the data.

4. **Serious harm declined for a decade and has risen since 2020.**
   Fatal and serious-injury collisions per 100,000 residents fell from **56.4 in 2010 to 19.2 in 2020**, then rose to
   **28.8 in 2023**, above the 2019 level (26.0). The **24 deaths in 2023** were the most since 2017.

5. **More pedestrians are being seriously hurt.**
   **78 pedestrians** were killed or seriously injured in 2023, more than double the 2020 figure (32) and the highest
   since 2014 (92).

## Why it matters for Vision Zero

Vision Zero measures success by the number of people killed or seriously injured, not by collision counts. On that
measure, the late-night hours, the chronic intersections and pedestrian safety are where effort is most likely to
reduce serious harm. The afternoon peak matters for volume and congestion, but it is not where severity is concentrated.

## Recommendations

1. **Put late-night hours at the centre of severity programs.** Schedule speed management, enforcement and
   impaired-driving prevention around midnight to 5 a.m. Ask Edmonton Police Service for collision-level data on these
   hours so the causes (speed, impairment, lighting) can be confirmed before money is committed.
2. **Run a safety review of the 23 chronic intersections, starting with 107 Avenue & 142 Street.** Its jump from 75 to
   169 collisions between 2022 and 2023 deserves a specific look (construction, signal or geometry changes, or
   changes in how collisions are reported). Pair counts with traffic volumes so sites are ranked by risk, not just busyness.
3. **Fix the data so trends can be trusted.** Keep one severity definition across the 2022 change to Collision
   Reporting Centres, and publish collision-level open data (date, time, location, severity). Both are needed to tell
   real change from reporting change and to measure whether interventions work.

## Limitations

- **Reporting change in September 2022.** Collision Reporting Centres replaced police reports. Minor-injury collisions
  rose from 8% to 38% of all collisions between 2021 and 2023, mostly through reclassification. Part of the 2022–23 rise in
  serious injuries may also reflect this change; fatalities are the measure least affected.
- **2020 is unusual** (the first pandemic year) and is not a good baseline.
- **Summary tables only.** The City publishes counts, not individual collisions, so causes, exact locations and
  contributing factors can't be analysed, and hotspots aren't adjusted for traffic volume.
- **Top-location lists cover only about the top 50 sites per year**; a site missing from a list is unknown, not zero.
- **The data runs to 2023**: as of October 2026, the open-data tables end at 2023.
